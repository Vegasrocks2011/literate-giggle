KS3 Biology — Core Knowledge

Cells and Organisation

Cell structure, organelles, diffusion, unicellular organisms, levels of organisation

Must know
Cell organelles and functions
  • Cell membrane — controls what enters/leaves
  • Nucleus — contains DNA, controls cell
  • Cytoplasm — where chemical reactions occur
  • Mitochondria — site of aerobic respiration (energy release)
  • Chloroplasts — site of photosynthesis (plant cells only)
  • Cell wall — support, made of cellulose (plant cells only)
  • Vacuole — storage, maintains cell shape (plant cells only)
Must know
Plant vs animal cells
Animal cells have: nucleus, cell membrane, cytoplasm, mitochondria.

Plant cells have all of the above PLUS: cell wall, chloroplasts, large vacuole.

Bacterial cells have no nucleus — DNA is free in the cytoplasm.
Must know
Levels of organisation
Cell → Tissue → Organ → Organ system → Organism. Each level is made of the level below working together. Example: muscle cells → muscle tissue → heart → circulatory system → human.
Should know
Diffusion
Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of high concentration to low concentration — down a concentration gradient. It is passive (requires no energy). Important in gas exchange (O₂ and CO₂) and nutrient absorption.

Skeletal and Muscular Systems

Functions of the skeleton, antagonistic muscles, biomechanics

Must know
Functions of the skeleton
  • Support — gives the body shape and holds organs in place
  • Protection — skull protects brain; ribcage protects heart and lungs
  • Movement — bones act as levers for muscles to act on
  • Blood cell production — red bone marrow makes red blood cells
Must know
Antagonistic muscles
Muscles can only pull, not push. They work in antagonistic pairs — when one contracts, the other relaxes. Example: bicep contracts → arm bends (flexion). Tricep contracts → arm straightens (extension). Tendons attach muscle to bone. Ligaments attach bone to bone.
Should know
Joints
Joints allow movement between bones. Synovial joints (e.g. knee, elbow) contain synovial fluid to reduce friction. Cartilage covers bone ends and acts as a shock absorber. Different joint types allow different ranges of movement.
Stretch
Biomechanics
The skeleton acts as a system of levers. The effort (from muscles), load (weight being moved) and fulcrum (pivot/joint) determine the mechanical advantage. Measuring force exerted by muscles uses spring balances or force sensors.

Nutrition and Digestion

Healthy diet, digestive system, enzymes, deficiency diseases

Must know
Food groups and their roles
  • Carbohydrates — energy source
  • Lipids (fats/oils) — energy store, insulation, cell membranes
  • Proteins — growth and repair
  • Vitamins — e.g. Vit C (scurvy prevention), Vit D (bone health)
  • Minerals — e.g. iron (haemoglobin), calcium (bones)
  • Fibre — aids gut movement
  • Water — chemical reactions, transport
Must know
The digestive system
Mouth → oesophagus → stomach → small intestine → large intestine → rectum → anus. Enzymes are biological catalysts that speed up digestion. Amylase breaks down starch. Protease breaks down protein. Lipase breaks down fat. Villi in the small intestine absorb nutrients into the blood.
Must know
Diet imbalances
  • Obesity — excess energy intake stored as fat; risk of diabetes, heart disease
  • Starvation — insufficient food; muscle wasting, organ failure
  • Scurvy — vitamin C deficiency
  • Rickets — vitamin D / calcium deficiency
  • Anaemia — iron deficiency (fewer red blood cells)
Should know
Bacteria in digestion
The gut contains billions of beneficial bacteria (gut microbiome). They help break down fibre, produce vitamins (e.g. Vitamin K), and protect against harmful bacteria. Antibiotics can disrupt the gut microbiome.

Gas Exchange

Structure of the lungs, breathing mechanism, stomata, exercise and health

Must know
The gas exchange system
Air passes through: nose/mouth → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli. Alveoli adaptations: large surface area, thin walls (one cell thick), moist lining, rich blood supply. O₂ diffuses into blood; CO₂ diffuses out.
Must know
Breathing mechanism
Inhaling: diaphragm contracts (flattens), intercostal muscles contract, ribcage moves up and out → volume increases → pressure decreases → air rushes in.
Exhaling: diaphragm relaxes (domes up), ribcage moves in and down → volume decreases → pressure increases → air pushed out.
Must know
Impacts on gas exchange
  • Exercise — increases breathing rate and depth; more O₂ needed for respiration
  • Asthma — airways narrow, making breathing difficult; treated with inhalers
  • Smoking — damages cilia, causes tar build-up, destroys alveoli (emphysema), leads to cancer
Should know
Stomata in plants
Stomata are tiny pores mainly on the underside of leaves, surrounded by guard cells. They open to allow CO₂ in for photosynthesis and O₂ out. They close to reduce water loss (transpiration) in hot/dry conditions.

Reproduction

Human reproduction, plant reproduction, fertilisation, dispersal

Must know
Human reproduction
Gametes: sperm (male) and egg (female). Fertilisation — fusion of sperm and egg → zygote → embryo → foetus. Gestation (pregnancy) ≈ 40 weeks. The placenta allows nutrients and O₂ to pass to the foetus and CO₂/waste to pass back. Maternal lifestyle (smoking, alcohol, drugs) affects foetal development.
Must know
The menstrual cycle
A monthly cycle (approximately 28 days) that prepares the uterus for pregnancy. Days 1–5: menstruation (uterus lining sheds). Days 6–13: lining rebuilds. Day 14: ovulation (egg released). Days 15–28: if unfertilised, lining breaks down again.
Must know
Plant reproduction
Flower parts: anther (makes pollen), stigma (receives pollen), ovary (contains ovules). Pollination: transfer of pollen from anther to stigma. Wind-pollinated: light pollen, no petals. Insect-pollinated: colourful petals, nectar, sticky pollen. After fertilisation → seed and fruit formation.
Should know
Seed dispersal
Seeds are dispersed to reduce competition. Methods: wind (maple, dandelion), animal (eaten — berry; carried — burdock), water (coconut), self-dispersal (explosive pods). Wider dispersal = less competition for light, water and nutrients.

Photosynthesis and Respiration

Word equations, reactants, products, aerobic and anaerobic

Must know
Photosynthesis
CO₂ + H₂O → glucose + O₂
Requires light energy. Occurs in chloroplasts. Leaf adaptations: broad (large surface area), thin (short diffusion distance), chlorophyll (absorbs light), stomata (gas exchange), veins (water and glucose transport).
Must know
Aerobic respiration
glucose + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O + energy
Occurs in mitochondria. Releases energy for all life processes — movement, growth, warmth. Used continuously by all living cells.
Must know
Anaerobic respiration
In humans (muscles): glucose → lactic acid + energy
Occurs when O₂ is insufficient (intense exercise). Lactic acid builds up causing muscle fatigue.
In yeast: glucose → ethanol + CO₂ + energy
Used in brewing and baking (fermentation).
Should know
Comparing aerobic and anaerobic
  • Aerobic: needs O₂, produces CO₂ + water, releases more energy
  • Anaerobic: no O₂ needed, produces lactic acid (animals) or ethanol + CO₂ (yeast), releases less energy
  • Anaerobic is unsustainable long-term — lactic acid causes pain and fatigue

Ecosystems and Interdependence

Food webs, interdependence, pollination, toxic accumulation, biodiversity

Must know
Food webs and energy flow
Producers (plants) → Primary consumers → Secondary consumers → Tertiary consumers. Arrows show the direction of energy flow (from eaten to eater). Removing one species affects all others — interdependence. Energy is lost at each level (as heat and movement).
Must know
Biodiversity
Biodiversity = variety of species in an ecosystem. High biodiversity → more stable ecosystem (if one species is lost, others can fill its role). Threats: habitat destruction, pollution, invasive species, climate change. Gene banks preserve genetic material of endangered species.
Should know
Insect pollination and food security
Many crops (fruits, vegetables, nuts) depend on insect pollination. Declining bee populations threaten global food security. Loss of pollinator habitat, pesticide use and disease all contribute. This links ecology directly to human food supply.
Stretch
Bioaccumulation of toxins
Toxic substances (e.g. mercury, DDT) are not broken down and accumulate in organisms. Concentrations increase at each trophic level (bioaccumulation) — top predators have the highest concentrations. Example: pesticides in insect → small bird → bird of prey.

Genetics and Evolution

DNA, genes, chromosomes, variation, natural selection, extinction

Must know
DNA, genes and chromosomes
DNA is a double helix molecule that carries genetic information. DNA is organised into genes (sections that code for a specific protein/characteristic). Genes are found on chromosomes, which are in the nucleus. Humans have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs). Watson, Crick, Wilkins and Franklin discovered the double helix structure of DNA.
Must know
Variation
Continuous variation: range of values, no distinct categories (e.g. height, mass, hand span). Shown as a bell curve. Discontinuous variation: distinct categories, no intermediate values (e.g. blood group, tongue rolling, sex). Variation is caused by genes, environment, or both.
Must know
Natural selection and evolution
1. Variation exists within a species. 2. More offspring produced than can survive. 3. Those with advantageous traits are more likely to survive and reproduce. 4. These traits are inherited by offspring. Over many generations → species change (evolution). Evidence: fossil record, DNA analysis, observable change.
Must know
Extinction
A species becomes extinct when all individuals die and it cannot reproduce. Causes: environmental change, habitat destruction, disease, predation, competition from invasive species. Climate change is increasingly a driver. Gene banks preserve genetic material to prevent total loss.

Health

Recreational drugs, substance misuse, effects on the body

Must know
Effects of recreational drugs
Drugs alter the way the body and brain work. Categories: stimulants (e.g. caffeine, nicotine — speed up CNS), depressants (e.g. alcohol — slow CNS), hallucinogens (e.g. cannabis — distort perception). All recreational drugs can harm health and behaviour. Many cause addiction.
Must know
Smoking and alcohol
Smoking: nicotine is addictive; tar causes cancer; carbon monoxide reduces O₂ in blood; damages cilia and alveoli. Alcohol: depresses the nervous system; damages liver (cirrhosis); impairs judgement and reaction time; addictive. Both harm the foetus if used during pregnancy.
Should know
Short and long-term effects
Short-term: impaired coordination, altered behaviour, euphoria, nausea. Long-term: addiction, organ damage, mental health problems, increased cancer risk, social and financial harm. The earlier drugs are used, the greater the impact on developing brains.
Stretch
Drugs and life processes
Drugs interfere with: nerve signal transmission (stimulants/depressants), cell respiration (carbon monoxide from smoking competes with O₂), organ function (alcohol and liver enzymes), and reproduction (smoking reduces fertility, affects foetal development).
